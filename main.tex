\begin{abstract}
Spontaneous symmetry breaking underlies the emergence of spatial order in condensed matter, occurring when a ground or thermal state has lower symmetry than its governing Hamiltonian. Wilczek proposed an analogous breaking of time translation symmetry, whereby a quantum system in its lowest-energy state would undergo persistent periodic motion. Although this equilibrium proposal was challenged, time crystalline order was subsequently formulated in nonequilibrium systems. In periodically driven systems, continuous time-translation symmetry reduces to a discrete symmetry, whose spontaneous breaking can produce a \emph{discrete time crystal} (DTC) with robust oscillations at an integer multiple of the drive period. Since generic driven systems absorb energy and heat toward infinite temperature, such order requires mechanisms including many body localization, prethermalization, or integrability to suppress heating. We review the Floquet algebraic origin of long lived oscillations and their dependence on driving protocols, initial states, and interactions, highlighting theoretical and experimental advances and the roles of symmetry, dimensionality, and dissipation in stabilizing DTCs.
\end{abstract}

\section{\label{sec:intro}Introduction}
%==============================================================
Symmetry is a central organizing principle of nature. In condensed matter, the spontaneous breaking of symmetry underlies the emergence of order. A ferromagnet breaks spin-rotation symmetry, while a crystal breaks continuous translational symmetry. In both cases, the ground state or thermal state exhibits less symmetry than the Hamiltonian from which it arises. The quest for a new phase of matter is paramount in order to development of science and progress of technology. 

In 2012, Wilczek asked whether \emph{time}-translation symmetry could be broken in classical as well as in domain of quantum mechanics. It is interesting the proposed time translational symmetry breaking(TTSB) was such that a quantum system in its lowest-energy state would move periodically forever~\cite{Wilczek2012,ShapereWilczek2012}. This directly questioned the laws of thermodynamics. Eventually, the proposal was quickly challenged and criticized broadly. Patrick Bruno~\cite{Bruno2013} and later Watanabe and Oshikawa~\cite{WatanabeOshikawa2015} showed that ground states and Gibbs states of local Hamiltonians cannot exhibit long-range order in time. This lead to a no-go theorem. Watanabe and Oshikawa defined this order using the correlator $\langle O(\mathbf{x},t)\,O(0,0)\rangle$, and later work made the result rigorous for Gibbs states~\cite{WatanabeOshikawaKoma2020}. 

However, the idea of legitimate TTSB was revived by considering the quantum system to be far from equilibrium. In a periodically driven many body quantum system, the Hamiltonian satisfies $H(t+T)=H(t)$, and only the \emph{discrete} time-translation symmetry $t\to t+T$ remains. However, if the state and corresponding observables respond with a period $mT$, where $m>1$ is an integer, the discrete time-translation symmetry is spontaneously broken. This is the essence of a \emph{discrete time crystal} (DTC)~\cite{Sacha2015,KhemaniPRL2016,ElseBauerNayak2016,vonKeyserlingk2016,YaoPRL2017}. The response must also be robust against perturbations and persist in the thermodynamic limit. Within a year of these proposals, signatures of period doubling were reported in trapped ions~\cite{Zhang2017} and in dense ensembles of nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centres~\cite{Choi2017}.

In this short review, we focus on the Floquet algebraic origin of long-lived oscillations and their dependence on driving protocols, initial states, and interactions. We highlight theoretical and experimental advances and the roles of symmetry, dimensionality, and dissipation in stabilizing DTCs. We restrict our attention to disorder-free, interacting spin-$1/2$ systems.  We also discuss the role of integrability in producing persistent oscillations, which is a prerequisite for time crystalline order.

\subsection{Background}
With the no-go theorem, the search for time crystals shifted to nonequilibrium systems. The first proposals were for periodically driven (Floquet) systems, which have discrete time-translation symmetry. In order to break this symmetry, the system must respond with a period that is an integer multiple of the drive period. To comprehend this, we first review the Floquet formalism. For a $T=2\pi/\omega_D$-periodic Hamiltonian, $\displaystyle H(t)=H(t+T)$, and the wavefunction $\ket{\psi(t)}$ obeys the Schr\"odinger equation $i\partial_t\ket{\psi(t)}=H(t)\ket{\psi(t)}$, to obtain the stroboscopic evolution associated with observables. This is time translation symmetry. 

However, if there exists an operator $\hat{\mathcal{O}}$ corresponding to a short range correlated wave function such that,
\begin{equation}
  \expval{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{\psi(nT)} = \expval{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{\psi(0)},
\end{equation}
where $n>0$ is an integer, then the system is said to have broken the discrete time translation symmetry. Then the system is expected to respond with a period $mT$, where $m>1$ is an integer. This manifest subharmonic reponses which can be readily observed in Fourier spectrum of the observable $\hat{\mathcal{O}}$. 

However, this does not guarantee the manifestation of a discrete time crystal. The response should emerge from the dynamics of the interacting driven system. Additionally, to label a system as a time crystal, it should follow certain criteria. The criteria for a discrete time crystal are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \renewcommand{\labelenumi}{\alph{enumi})}
  \item The system should exhibit spontaneous breaking of discrete time-translation symmetry: a local observable $f(t)$ should satisfy $f(t) \neq f(t+T)$, even though the Hamiltonian is periodic, $\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}(t+T)$.
  \item The response should exhibit robust subharmonic oscillations without fine-tuning the system parameters and should remain stable under perturbations.
  \item The oscillations should persist indefinitely in the thermodynamic limit, $N \to \infty$.
\end{enumerate}
Therefore, the dynamics of a periodically driven interacting system must support a robust subharmonic response that persists in the thermodynamic limit. In generic driven systems, energy absorption eventually leads to heating toward infinite temperature due to ETH(Eigenstate thermaliation hypothesis)~\cite{Deutsch1991ETH, Srednicki1994ETH, DAlessio2016ETH}, thereby destroying this response. A central challenge is therefore to identify mechanisms that suppress heating while stabilizing the subharmonic dynamics. One widely studied approach is to localize the many-body system throughout the drive cycle.

Several theoretically and experimentally established mechanisms can provide such localization or otherwise suppress heating. The most widely used approach is to introduce strong disorder, which can produce many-body localization (MBL)~\cite{Basko2006,PalHuse2010}. MBL inhibits energy absorption from the drive and thus prevents heating to infinite temperature. However, it is vulnerable to external perturbations that can induce delocalization and ultimately destroy the localized phase. A second approach is to use a clean, disorder-free system driven at high frequency. Such a system can enter a prethermal regime in which its dynamics are governed, for long but finite times, by an effective time-independent Hamiltonian before eventual heating occurs~\cite{Ponte2015, Mori2016}. Additionally, precise engineering in drive protocols can nullify the dynamics of the system, thereby suppressing the thermalization and eventually produce a prethermal regime~\cite{Banerjee_2025}. A further approach is to consider an integrable system whose dynamics are constrained by an extensive set of conserved quantities. Under appropriate conditions, integrability can support persistent oscillations and prevent heating to infinite temperature~\cite{Medenjak2020PRB}.

The next section introduces the generic drive protocol for a discrete time crystal. We then review established results on the mechanisms that produce robust discrete time-crystalline order in such systems.

\subsection{Discrete time crystal}
\label{sec:dtc}
The canonical protocol for realizing a discrete time crystal is based on a binary Floquet drive. During each period, the system evolves under two Hamiltonians, $\hat{H}_1$ and $\hat{H}_2$, for durations $T_1$ and $T_2=T-T_1$, respectively. The one-period Floquet operator is therefore
\begin{equation}
  \hat{U}_F = \exp(-i \hat{H}_2 T_2) \exp(-i \hat{H}_1 T_1).
  \label{eq:binary_floquet}
\end{equation}
The first step is chosen to implement an approximately global spin flip, while the second is generated by an interacting local or quasi-local Hamiltonian. For a spin-$1/2$ chain, a representative choice is
\begin{equation}
  \hat{H}_1=g\sum_i\hat{\sigma}_i^x,
  \qquad
  \hat{H}_2=\sum_i\left(J_i\hat{\sigma}_i^z\hat{\sigma}_{i+1}^z
  +h_i\hat{\sigma}_i^z\right),
\end{equation}
where $gT_1 = \pi/2(1-\varepsilon_r)$, $J_i$ and $h_i$ denote random couplings and longitudinal fields, $\varepsilon_r$ is spin rotation error, which is zero for ideal pulses. Defining the two parts of the drive as
\begin{equation}
  \hat{U}_1=e^{-i\hat{H}_1T_1},
  \qquad
  \hat{U}_2=e^{-i\hat{H}_2T_2},
  \qquad
  \hat{U}_F=\hat{U}_2\hat{U}_1.
\end{equation}
the first step flips every spin,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{U}_1^\dagger\hat{\sigma}_i^z\hat{U}_1=-\hat{\sigma}_i^z,
  \qquad
  \hat{U}_1^\dagger\left(\sum_i\hat{\sigma}_i^z\right)\hat{U}_1
  =-\sum_i\hat{\sigma}_i^z,
\end{equation}
whereas the disordered interacting evolution generated by $\hat{H}_2$ can support many-body localization and thereby inhibit heating. The resulting stroboscopic magnetization is
\begin{equation}
  M(nT)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_i
  \langle\psi_0|\hat{U}_F^{-n}\hat{\sigma}_i^z\hat{U}_F^n|\psi_0\rangle.
\end{equation}
In the ideal pulse limit, this magnetization alternates between successive periods,
\begin{equation}
  M(nT)\simeq(-1)^nM(0),
  \qquad
  M((n+2)T)=M(nT).
\end{equation}
Thus, the observable responds with period $2T$, rather than with the drive period $T$, signaling the subharmonic response associated with discrete time-translation-symmetry breaking. In a genuine discrete time-crystalline phase, this response must remain stable against perturbations and persist indefinitely in the thermodynamic limit.
\begin{figure}[]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/dtc_fig_bc.pdf}
  \caption{(a) Stroboscopic magnetization $M(nT) = \langle\hat{\sigma}_i^z(nT)\rangle$, showing period doubling at $\varepsilon_r=0.1$ for interacting systems($J_i>0$) and non-interacting systems($J_i=0$). (b) Fourier spectrum of the magnetization, showing a sharp peak at $\omega=\pi/T$.}
  \label{fig:dtc_fig_b}
\end{figure}

Experimentally, the global spin flip is implemented by a nominal $\pi$ pulse, which is generally imperfect. We parametrize the pulse imperfection by writing
\begin{equation}
  \hat{H}_1 = g \sum_i \hat{\sigma}^x_i,
\end{equation}
where $g = \frac{\pi}{2T_1} (1-\varepsilon_r)$ and $\varepsilon_r$ is the pulse imperfection parameter.
\begin{equation}
  \hat{H}_1 = \frac{\pi}{2T_1} (1-\varepsilon_r) \sum_i \hat{\sigma}^x_i.
\end{equation}
The defining feature of the discrete time-crystalline phase is that the subharmonic response remains stable over a finite interval of $\varepsilon_r$, rather than occurring only at the fine-tuned value $\varepsilon_r=0$. This robustness arises from the interacting dynamics during the $T_2$ interval, provided that heating is suppressed and the time-crystalline order is preserved. This can be seen in the Fig.~\ref{fig:dtc_fig_b}, where the subharmonic response is robust against perturbations in the pulse imperfection $\varepsilon_r$. It is interesting that the in presence of the interaction term,$J_i>0$ only the FFT spectrum of the magnetization shows a sharp peak at $\omega=\pi/T$, where at the nil interaction limit, $J_i=0$, the peak is shifted from $\omega=\pi/T$ to $\omega=\pi(1-\varepsilon_r)/T$. This shows that the interaction term couteract against the phase difference introduced by the finite spin rotational error $\varepsilon_r$. Therefore, the interaction term is crucial for the emergence as well as stabilization of the subharmonic response. The robustness of the subharmonic response against perturbations in $\varepsilon_r$ is a defining feature of the discrete time-crystalline phase. The disorder induced MBL and consequently obtaining rigid DTC is theoretically \todo{put corresponding reference} as well as experimentally \todo{put corresponding reference} well established. 


However, quenched disorder is not essential for realizing DTC order. We therefore turn to clean periodically driven systems, where dynamical many-body localization (DMBL) can suppress heating through the intrinsic dynamics and provide a route toward time-crystalline behavior in the absence of disorder. A representative example is an Ising chain subject to a time-dependent transverse field,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{H}_2(t) = \sum_{i} J_{ij} \hat{\sigma}_i^y \hat{\sigma}_j^y - h\sin(\omega t) \sum_i \hat{\sigma}_i^z,
\end{equation}
where $J_{ij} = \frac{J_0}{|i-j|^\alpha}$. As demonstrated in Ref.~\cite{rahaman2024prb, rahaman2024njp}, this model exhibits robust dynamical localization when the drive parameters satisfy
$\mathcal{J}_0\left(\frac{4h}{\omega}\right) = 0$, with $\mathcal{J}_0$ denoting the zeroth-order Bessel function of the first kind. This localization mechanism can then be combined with a binary Floquet protocol, analogous to that introduced in Eq.~\ref{eq:binary_floquet}, to stabilize a subharmonic response and thereby realize DTC order in a clean system.

A complementary route to realizing DTC order without disorder is provided by high-frequency driving. When the drive frequency $\omega_D$ is much larger than the local energy scale $J_{\rm loc}$, absorbing a single drive quantum requires a high-order many-body process. Consequently, the heating time is exponentially long~\cite{AbaninCMP2017,KuwaharaMoriSaito2016}:
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\rm heat}\gtrsim\exp\big(c\,\omega_D/J_{\rm loc}\big).
\end{equation}
For times shorter than $\tau_{\rm heat}$, a suitable rotating-frame transformation produces an effective static Hamiltonian with an \emph{emergent} $\mathbb{Z}_2$ symmetry. If the corresponding prethermal state spontaneously breaks this symmetry, the discrete time-translation symmetry is broken and the laboratory-frame observable exhibits a robust period-doubled response~\cite{ElseBauerNayakPRX2017}. Thus, the existence of a prethermal DTC is constrained by the same equilibrium symmetry-breaking considerations discussed above: for short-range interactions, finite-temperature breaking of a discrete symmetry requires $d\ge 2$, although sufficiently long-range interactions can modify this conclusion.

Another route to realizing DTC order in a clean system is provided by a two-tone drive protocol that dynamically cancels the interaction contribution to the Hamiltonian, results in a flat quasienergy band structure. In this scheme, the system is subjected to two drives with commensurate frequencies, $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$, satisfying $\omega_1=n\omega_2$, where $n>1$ is an odd integer~\cite{Banerjee_2025}. The corresponding time-dependent Hamiltonian is
\begin{multline}
  \hat{H}_2(t) = \sum_{i} J \hat{\sigma}_i^x \hat{\sigma}_j^x + h\Sgn[\sin(\omega_1 t)] \sum_i \hat{\sigma}_i^z\\ - h\Sgn[\sin(\omega_2 t)] \sum_i \hat{\sigma}_i^z,
\end{multline}
where $\Sgn$ denotes the sign function. For the commensurate choice of drive frequencies specified above, the two-tone protocol dynamically cancels the interaction contribution over a driving cycle. The resulting flat quasienergy bands produce dynamical localization and thereby suppress heating. When combined with a binary Floquet protocol that includes a spin-flip operation, this localization mechanism can stabilize a subharmonic response and realize DTC order in a clean system. Small residual interactions can further stabilize the response against spin-rotation errors, which act as external perturbations. Rahaman et al.~\cite{rahaman2024prb} showed that the resulting stroboscopic magnetization can exhibit a robust period-doubled response with period $2T$. Thus, this protocol provides a route to realizing clean DTC order without relying on quenched disorder or prethermalization.

\section{\label{sec:integrability}Integrability and the (usual) absence of oscillations}
%==============================================================
An integrable many-body system has an extensive set of local or quasi-local conserved charges $\{Q_k\}$ with $[H,Q_k]=[Q_k,Q_l]=0$~\cite{EsslerFagotti2016,IlievskiQuasilocal2016}. Free-fermion models such as the transverse-field Ising chain~\cite{LSM1961,Pfeuty1970} belong to this class, as do interacting Bethe-ansatz-solvable models such as the XXZ and Hubbard chains. Because these charges constrain the dynamics, integrable systems do not thermalize. After a quench they relax locally to a generalized Gibbs ensemble (GGE)~\cite{Rigol2007,VidmarRigol2016,Ilievski2015GGE}
\begin{equation}
\rho_{\rm GGE}=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\Big(-\sum_k\lambda_kQ_k\Big),
\end{equation}
where the Lagrange multipliers $\lambda_k$ are fixed by the initial values $\langle Q_k\rangle$. The quantum Newton's cradle~\cite{Kinoshita2006} demonstrates this memory of initial conditions. Periodically driven integrable systems similarly relax to a periodic GGE~\cite{LazaridesDasMoessner2014PRL}.

Note that integrable systems \emph{do} relax, just not to a thermal state. Expand the state in energy eigenstates, $\ket{\psi}=\sum_m c_m\ket{m}$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dephasing}
\begin{split}
\langle O(t)\rangle={}&\sum_m|c_m|^2O_{mm}\\
&+\sum_{m\neq n}c_m^*c_n\,O_{mn}\,e^{i(E_m-E_n)t}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
In the thermodynamic limit, the energy differences $E_m-E_n$ form a continuum. The second term then dephases by the Riemann--Lebesgue lemma, leaving only the diagonal (GGE) part. Conservation laws determine \emph{where} the system relaxes, but they do not by themselves prevent relaxation. Persistent oscillations require more: the spectrum must contain an extensive set of exactly commensurate energy differences, and these differences must contribute finite weight to local observables. \section{\label{sec:experiments}Experimental realizations}
%==============================================================
The first observations used two complementary platforms. Zhang \emph{et al.}~\cite{Zhang2017} drove a chain of ten trapped ions with long-range Ising interactions and engineered disorder, observing period-doubled magnetization that remained locked as pulse imperfections varied. Additionally, Choi \emph{et al.}~\cite{Choi2017} used about a million NV centres with disordered dipolar interactions, where slow, critical thermalization extended the lifetime of order. NMR experiments in ordered dipolar crystals later showed DTC signatures without disorder~\cite{Rovny2018}, pointing to prethermal or long-range mechanisms. A prethermal DTC with an exponentially long lifetime set by the drive frequency was demonstrated with trapped ions~\cite{Kyprianidis2021}.

Later experiments addressed eigenstate order directly. Randall \emph{et al.}~\cite{Randall2021} used a controllable register of $^{13}$C nuclear spins around an NV centre to probe an MBL DTC. Mi \emph{et al.}~\cite{Mi2022} used a superconducting quantum processor to show that time-crystalline order persists across the spectrum, including random initial states, with a protocol designed to separate intrinsic decay from decoherence. A 57-qubit realization on a cloud quantum computer followed~\cite{FreyRachel2022}. Magnon condensates in superfluid $^3$He show related behaviour~\cite{Autti2018}.

Two points matter in this chapter. Floquet circuits on superconducting processors closely match the kicked Ising chain of Eq.~\eqref{eq:UF}, with tunable integrability-breaking terms, so its free-fermion limit and edge-versus-bulk dichotomy are directly testable. The phantom-helix experiments~\cite{Jepsen2022} probe the dynamical-symmetry physics of Section~\ref{sec:dynsym}. In all platforms, lifetimes are limited by decoherence, and separating intrinsic persistence (C3) from experimental limitations remains a central challenge.

\section{\label{sec:outlook}Open problems and outlook}
%==============================================================
Integrability gives an exact, analytically controlled view of time crystals. It shows that persistent oscillations require commensurate spectral structure, expressed as dynamical symmetries and $\pi$-modes, and that this structure must be protected by the drive and stabilized against domain-wall motion. Several directions remain open.

\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{Clean interacting systems.} Is there a rigorous, non-perturbative example of a DTC in a clean, short-range, one-dimensional system that persists indefinitely? Almost strong $\pi$-modes and long-range interactions are the leading candidates.
\item \textbf{Robust dynamical symmetries.} Can quasi-local dynamical symmetries like those in the XXZ chain be made robust, for instance through drive-induced locking, so that Hamiltonian-determined frequencies become rigid?
\item \textbf{Between integrable and chaotic.} Quantum many-body scars~\cite{Turner2018,SerbynScarsReview2021} and Hilbert-space fragmentation~\cite{Sala2020} generate long-lived oscillations from \emph{restricted} algebraic structure. Their connection to dynamical symmetries suggests a single framework for non-stationarity~\cite{Buca2022SciPost}.
\item \textbf{Beyond period doubling.} Higher-order and fractional responses~\cite{Pizzi2021}, along with time quasicrystals~\cite{Autti2018}, remain largely unexplored in integrable settings.
\item \textbf{Asymptotic status of MBL.} Resolving the avalanche question~\cite{DeRoeckHuveneers2017,Morningstar2022} will determine whether disordered DTCs are true phases or merely extremely long-lived crossovers.
\end{enumerate}

Time crystals show that \emph{phase of matter} matters far from equilibrium, and integrable models most clearly show what a time crystal needs and why generic systems struggle to provide it.
